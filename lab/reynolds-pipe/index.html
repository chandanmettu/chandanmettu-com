Reynolds' pipe

When does a smooth flow turn chaotic? In 1883 Osborne Reynolds let water run from a still tank through a glass tube and fed a fine thread of dye into its mouth. While the water ran slowly the thread stayed straight. Opened past a certain speed, it flashed into eddies and filled the tube. What decides it is one number, Re = ρVD/μ: the water's inertia over its viscosity.

1. Predict

Before you open the valve, what will the dye do?

2. Try

Use the bench controls: the outlet valve, the water temperature and how long the tank has settled. The live readings are below.

Why

Below about Re 2,000 any disturbance dies away and the thread stays straight. Above it a disturbance can grow into a puff of eddies, and the puffs join up as the speed rises. How far above depends on how still the water is: stirred, the tank trips near 2,000; left for hours, Reynolds kept the thread straight to about 13,000.

3. Challenge

Make the dye thread break up while Re is still below 3,000. Hint: a still tank holds out a long time.

The model

Tube: ½ inch bore, 1.5 m long, 0.5 m of head, piezometer taps at 0.3 m and 1.2 m. The flow comes from one energy balance, H = (1 + K_in + fL/D + K_valve) V²/2g, with f = (1 − γ)·64/Re + γ·0.316 Re^−¼ and viscosity from the Vogel equation. Transition is a one-dimensional intermittency field carried with the flow. A teaching model of transition, not a turbulence simulation.
