Notes, model and sources

Handwheel opens the valve and sets the flow. Piezometers read the pressure head at two taps 0.9 m apart. The bench rule has a tick every 5 cm along the 1.5 m tube. The tube is drawn about five times wider than its ½-inch bore so the dye reads; every number is for the real tube.

Three things to try

  1. Open the valve slowly from about 40 %. The dye stays a thread until Re passes the value this tank trips at, then it starts flashing a few tens of diameters down the tube, just as Reynolds described.
  2. Press Stir the tank and repeat. With the water disturbed the tube trips near 2,000–3,000. Left to settle for hours, Reynolds held it laminar to about 13,000. Below 2,000 nothing you do keeps it turbulent.
  3. Warm the water. ν halves between 20 and 50 °C, so the same valve setting doubles Re. Reynolds noticed exactly this when the water in his tank warmed.

The model

The flow comes from one energy balance between the tank surface and the free jet, using the friction factor of whatever is in the tube at that moment. The piezometer heads come from the same balance, so the chart point is measured from the drawn columns.

H = (1 + Kin + f L/D + Kvalve) V²/2g   f = (1 − γ)·64/Re + γ·0.316 Re−1/4   Re = ρVD/μ(T)

Tube: ½ inch bore, 1.5 m long, 0.5 m of head, taps at 0.3 m and 1.2 m. Viscosity from the Vogel equation (1.002 mPa·s at 20 °C). Transition is a one-dimensional intermittency field γ(x) carried with the flow: every patch decays below Re 2,000, puffs from inlet disturbances survive above it and grow into slugs above about 2,300, and above the tank's onset value the flow trips by itself near the inlet. This is a teaching model of transition, not a turbulence simulation; the laminar and Blasius laws it blends are the real ones. The tube is drawn about five times wider than its true bore.
