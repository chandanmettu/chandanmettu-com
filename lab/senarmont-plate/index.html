Sénarmont's plate

In 1847 Henri de Sénarmont coated crystal plates in wax, touched a hot wire to the centre and watched the melt spread. In glass it spread as a circle. In quartz cut along its c axis it spread as an ellipse, the first direct sight of conductivity as a tensor.

Three things to try

  1. Pick Quartz, Y-cut and press Fresh wax. While the front grows, a/b sits at 1.29, which is √(10.4/6.21). Sénarmont measured 1.31. Near steady state the insulated rim stretches it a little further.
  2. Drag the probe around the plate. The lit needle (heat flux) points straight out from the stylus; the other needle (−∇T) stands square to the isotherm. They agree only on the two crystal axes, and the gap peaks near 15° for quartz. Near the rim the insulated edge turns the flux to run along it, and the solver parts company with the unbounded-plate formula.
  3. Turn the stage. The ellipse turns with the crystal, and the lab-frame tensor grows off-diagonal terms: a gradient along x now drives heat along y as well.

The model

A 0.3 mm plate, 40 mm across, on three standoffs in still air. The stylus holds a 0.6 mm spot at its set temperature, and the solver counts every joule it takes to do that. Wax melts at 62 °C (beeswax). The wax film's own heat capacity and latent heat are left out.

ρ cp d ∂T/∂t = d ∇·(K ∇T) − 2h (T − T∞)   q = −K ∇T   Klab = R(θ) diag(k₁, k₂) R(θ)ᵀ

Finite volumes on a 121 × 121 grid in the crystal's own frame, h = 12 W/m²·K per face, ambient 22 °C. Isotherms on the plate are drawn every 20 K. With K = diag(k₁, k₂) the field depends on x²/k₁ + y²/k₂ alone, so isotherms are ellipses with axes in the ratio √(k₁/k₂), and q = −K∇T comes out radial.
